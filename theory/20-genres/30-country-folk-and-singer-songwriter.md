# Country, Folk and Singer-Songwriter

**Identity:** the song is the whole point. Arrangement, production and
performance exist to deliver a lyric and a melody.

## Numbers

| Style | Tempo | Meter | Key |
|---|---|---|---|
| Folk / singer-songwriter | 70–120 | 4/4, 3/4, 6/8 | Major, guitar-friendly (G, D, C, A, E) |
| Country (classic) | 90–140 | 4/4, 3/4 | Major |
| Bluegrass | 120–180 | 4/4, 2/4 | Major, G/A/B |
| Country-pop | 90–130 | 4/4 | Major |
| Americana / alt-country | 70–130 | 4/4, 6/8 | Major and minor |
| Celtic folk | 100–180 | 4/4, 6/8, 9/8 | Modal (Dorian, Mixolydian) |

## Harmony

Simple, functional and diatonic:

| Progression | Use |
|---|---|
| `I–IV–V–I` | The foundation |
| `I–V–vi–IV` | Modern country/folk-pop |
| `I–IV–I–V` | Traditional |
| `I–vi–IV–V` | Ballads |
| `vi–IV–I–V` | Contemporary |
| `I–bVII–IV–I` | Mixolydian, folk-rock, Celtic |
| `i–bVII–bVI–bVII` | Modal folk |
| 12-bar blues | Country blues, rockabilly |

Colour moves used sparingly:
- **The "Nashville number system"**: chords notated by scale degree, so a song
  can be transposed instantly for any singer. Write charts as `1 4 5 6-`.
- **Sus4 resolving to the triad** — the guitar-idiomatic decoration.
- **Add9 and sus2** from open guitar voicings.
- **The bVII** from Mixolydian.
- **A borrowed iv** for the emotional moment.

## Instrumentation

| Instrument | Role |
|---|---|
| **Acoustic guitar** | The rhythmic and harmonic foundation; strumming or fingerpicking |
| **Bass (upright or electric)** | Roots and fifths; simple |
| **Drums** | Understated; brushes, rimshots, train beat |
| **Pedal steel** | Sustained swells and bends between chords; the country signifier |
| **Fiddle** | Melodic fills, double stops, drone strings |
| **Banjo** | Rolling patterns (bluegrass) or clawhammer (old-time) |
| **Dobro / resonator** | Slide fills |
| **Mandolin** | Chop on 2 and 4 (bluegrass) or tremolo melodies |
| **Piano** | Chordal support, honky-tonk fills |
| **Harmonica** | Melodic fills and solos |
| **Vocal harmony** | Thirds above, or high-lonesome above the melody |

## Guitar patterns

**Strumming** (D = down, U = up, `-` = miss):
```
Basic:      D - D U - U D U
Folk:       D - D U - U D U   (the "campfire" pattern)
Country:    D - D - D - D -   with alternating bass notes
```

**Travis picking** (thumb alternates bass notes, fingers pick melody):
```
thumb:  x - x - x - x -     (roots and fifths on 1, 2, 3, 4)
finger: - x - x x - x x
```

**Boom-chick** (country): bass note on 1 and 3, chord strum on 2 and 4.

## Drums

```
Train beat (country):
kick:   x - - - | - - - - | x - - - | - - - -
snare:  . x . x | . x . x | . x . x | . x . x    (constant 16ths, accents on 2 and 4)

Folk/ballad:
kick:   x - - - | - - - - | x - - - | - - - -
snare:  - - - - | x - - - | - - - - | x - - -
```
Brushes rather than sticks for ballads. The drums stay out of the way.

## Song structure

```
Intro (4-8) – Verse 1 – Chorus – Verse 2 – Chorus – Bridge – Chorus – Outro
```
or, for narrative songs:
```
Verse 1 – Verse 2 – Chorus – Verse 3 – Chorus – Verse 4 (resolution) – Chorus
```

**Country's structural convention:** the title is usually the last line of the
chorus, and often a play on words that recontextualises the verse.

## Lyrics

The lyric is the primary content, more than in any other genre covered here.

- **Concrete, specific detail.** Brand names, place names, times of day.
- **A story with a turn.** The last verse changes the meaning of the first.
- **Plain language.** No inversion, no poetic archaism.
- **The hook is a phrase people would say**, given a twist.
- Rhyme: mostly perfect rhyme; AABB or ABAB.
- Verse-chorus contrast: verse = the story, chorus = the feeling.

## Vocal harmony

- **Thirds above** the melody: the standard country/folk harmony.
- **Sixths below**.
- **High harmony above the third** ("high lonesome" in bluegrass).
- Family-band blend: singers matching vowel shapes and vibrato.
- In bluegrass, the harmony parts are as rhythmically precise as the lead.

## Production notes

- **Space and clarity over density.** A folk record has 4–7 elements.
- Record real instruments if at all possible; the character of wood and string
  is not replaceable by samples in this context.
- Acoustic guitar: two mics (or one at the 12th fret), high-passed at 80–100 Hz,
  a small cut at 200–300 Hz to remove boom.
- Vocal: forward, intimate, with modest compression. Breaths left in.
- Reverb: a plate or small hall, moderate. Not a modern dry pop sound.
- Dynamic range preserved: master to −12 to −10 LUFS.

## Signature techniques

- **The lyric leads.** Arrangement, production and performance exist to deliver
  it; anything competing with the words comes out.
- **Alternating bass (boom-chick)** — root on 1 and 3, chord on 2 and 4.
- **Travis picking**: the thumb keeps an independent bass pattern while the
  fingers carry the melody.
- **Pedal steel swells between chords**, arriving after the chord change rather
  than with it.
- **Harmony in thirds above**, or sixths below; high harmony above that for
  bluegrass.
- **The Nashville number system** — write charts by scale degree so the key can
  move to fit a singer.
- **Space over layers.** Four to seven elements is a full arrangement.
- **Leave the strum's timing alone**; its unevenness is the groove.

## Hazards

- Overproduction: adding layers because the arrangement feels empty, when the
  emptiness is the point.
- A lyric with no specifics.
- Quantised acoustic guitar; the strum's timing is the groove.
- Drums too loud for the genre.
- Vocal tuning that removes the performance.
- Complicated harmony; these genres are strengthened by simplicity.
