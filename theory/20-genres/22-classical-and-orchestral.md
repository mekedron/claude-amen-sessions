# Classical, Orchestral and Common-Practice Writing

**Identity:** 400 years of accumulated technique for writing multi-voice music
for acoustic instruments. Still the source of most harmonic and formal
vocabulary in Western music.

## The eras and what each contributes

| Era | Dates | Harmony | Texture | What to steal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Medieval** | 500–1400 | Modal, parallel fifths, drones | Monophonic, organum | Drones, modality, parallel motion |
| **Renaissance** | 1400–1600 | Modal, triadic, consonant | Polyphonic, imitative | Voice leading, imitation, choral spacing |
| **Baroque** | 1600–1750 | Functional tonality established; figured bass | Contrapuntal, continuous motion | Sequences, counterpoint, ostinato basses, motoric rhythm |
| **Classical** | 1750–1820 | Clear functional harmony, simple textures | Melody + accompaniment, periodic phrasing | Phrase structure, form, clarity, cadence types |
| **Romantic** | 1820–1900 | Chromaticism, modulation, extended harmony | Rich, dense, expressive | Chromatic mediants, thematic transformation, orchestration |
| **Impressionist** | 1890–1920 | Modes, whole tone, parallel chords, colour over function | Wash, timbre-focused | Non-functional harmony, parallel voicings, pentatonic/whole-tone colour |
| **20th c. modernist** | 1900–1975 | Atonality, serialism, polytonality | Fragmented, extreme | Cluster harmony, extended technique, rhythmic complexity |
| **Minimalism** | 1960– | Static, diatonic, slowly shifting | Repetitive, phasing | Additive process, phasing, pulse |
| **Contemporary / neo** | 1975– | Everything | Everything | Post-minimalism, spectralism, film-score hybrid |

## Forms

| Form | Structure | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Binary** | A B (usually repeated) | Dances, short pieces |
| **Rounded binary** | A B A' | Minuets |
| **Ternary** | A B A | Da capo arias, character pieces |
| **Sonata form** | Exposition (theme 1 in I, transition, theme 2 in V/III) — Development (fragmentation, modulation) — Recapitulation (both themes in I) — Coda | First movements; the dominant form of 1750–1900 |
| **Rondo** | A B A C A (B A) | Finales |
| **Theme & variations** | A A' A'' A''' | Any |
| **Fugue** | Subject — answer (in the dominant) — countersubject — episodes — stretto — pedal — final statement | Baroque, and any contrapuntal texture |
| **Passacaglia/chaconne** | Repeating bass or chord cycle with variations above | Loop-based music's ancestor |
| **Through-composed** | No repetition | Songs, tone poems |

### Phrase structure

- **Period**: 4-bar antecedent ending on a half cadence + 4-bar consequent
  ending on an authentic cadence. Question and answer.
- **Sentence**: 2-bar basic idea + 2-bar repetition (often transposed) + 4-bar
  continuation and cadence. Statement, restatement, development.
- **Hypermeter**: bars group into 4s and 8s exactly as in pop music.

## Counterpoint essentials

The species discipline, compressed:

1. **Consonances on strong beats**: unison, 3rd, 5th, 6th, octave. The 4th is a
   dissonance when it involves the bass.
2. **No parallel fifths or octaves** between any two voices.
3. **Prefer contrary motion**; use similar motion sparingly; never approach a
   perfect consonance by similar motion in the outer voices ("direct fifths").
4. **Dissonances are prepared and resolved**: suspensions (held from the previous
   chord, resolving down by step) are the main expressive device.
5. **Each voice is singable**: mostly steps, leaps followed by step in the
   opposite direction, a clear high point once per line.
6. **Voices stay in range** and do not cross.
7. Cadences: penultimate chord V, leading tone resolves up, 7th resolves down.

### Orchestration principles

- **Doubling at the octave** strengthens without thickening; doubling in unison
  changes timbre.
- **Spacing**: wide at the bottom, close at the top, exactly as in `../00-foundations/07-voice-leading.md`.
- **Weight**: brass > woodwind > strings at equal dynamics. To balance a melody
  in the woodwinds against strings, either thin the strings or double the
  woodwinds.
- **Register defines character** more than instrument choice: any instrument in
  its extreme high register sounds strained and tense; any in its low register
  sounds dark and rich.
- **Blend**: horns blend with everything. Flutes and clarinets blend. Oboes and
  trumpets do not blend — they lead.
- **Divisi strings** create the "wall of sound"; a single soloist creates
  intimacy. The change between them is a dramatic device.
- **Tutti** (everyone) is only powerful if used rarely.

## Minimalism (the direct ancestor of electronic loop music)

| Technique | Description |
|---|---|
| **Additive process** | Add one note to a repeating figure each cycle |
| **Subtractive process** | Remove notes progressively |
| **Phasing** | Two identical loops at slightly different tempos drift apart and realign |
| **Pulse** | A constant subdivision underlying everything |
| **Slow harmonic rhythm** | One chord for minutes |
| **Interlocking patterns** | Parts that fit into each other's gaps |
| **Gradual timbral change** | Instruments enter and leave a static texture |

## Writing "classical-sounding" music programmatically

1. Choose a form and a phrase length (8-bar periods).
2. Write a melody with a clear contour and one climax.
3. Harmonise it functionally: T–S–D–T with cadences at phrase ends.
4. Voice-lead the inner parts by minimal motion, contrary to the bass.
5. Give the bass a purposeful line (often stepwise or in fifths).
6. Add a non-chord tone vocabulary: passing tones, suspensions, appoggiaturas.
7. Orchestrate: melody in one register, bass in another, harmony filling
   between, with attention to doubling and weight.
8. Vary dynamics constantly — a flat dynamic is the giveaway of machine writing.

## Hazards

- Parallel fifths and octaves in a texture that is trying to sound contrapuntal.
- Chords in root position throughout — no bass line.
- All voices moving in the same rhythm (that is a chorale, not counterpoint).
- Writing outside instrument ranges, or in a register where the instrument
  cannot be heard against the others.
- No dynamic or articulation variation.
- MIDI orchestral mockups without note-length variation, velocity shaping and
  round-robin timbre changes sound like an organ, not an orchestra.
