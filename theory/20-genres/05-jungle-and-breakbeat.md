# Jungle and Breakbeat

**Identity:** the sampled funk break as the primary instrument. Chopped,
re-sequenced, pitched, time-stretched and layered, over a sub bass borrowed from
reggae soundsystem culture.

## Jungle

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Tempo | 155–175 (classic 160–170) |
| Meter | 4/4 |
| Key | Minor; often just a sub bass on one or two notes |
| Era | 1992–1997 UK, and its ongoing revival |
| Loudness | −9 to −7 LUFS |

### The break

The genre is defined by four or five specific recordings:

| Break | Source | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Amen** | The Winstons, "Amen, Brother" (1969) | The canonical break; 4 bars, ghost-note-rich, a shifted final bar |
| **Think** | Lyn Collins, "Think (About It)" (1972) | Bright, snappy, the "woo-yeah" is from the same record |
| **Apache** | Incredible Bongo Band (1973) | Bongo-heavy, open |
| **Funky Drummer** | James Brown (1970) | Ghost-note masterclass, laid back |
| **Hot Pants** | Bobby Byrd (1971) | Punchy |
| **Soul Pride** | James Brown (1969) | Loose, funky |

Technique:

1. **Slice** the break into 16ths (or by transient), then re-order the slices.
2. **Pitch** the whole break up by speeding it (the old way — pitch and tempo
   rise together) or time-stretch it (the modern way, which leaves the classic
   "stretched" artefacts when done crudely — an artefact that became an aesthetic).
3. **Layer** two or three different breaks so their ghost notes interleave.
4. **Reverse** individual slices for fills.
5. **Filter** entire bars for arrangement (a low-passed break in the intro, full
   break at the drop).
6. **Chop patterns** work best when the kick and snare stay near their expected
   positions and the *ghost notes* are what get scrambled.

### Bass

- Huge, clean sine sub, MIDI 26–36.
- Sparse: one or two long notes per bar, often on beat 1 and the "and" of 3.
- Reggae-derived: the bass plays around the beat, not on it.
- Occasional pitch slides between notes.

### Sound palette

Ragga MC vocals, dub sirens, reggae organ stabs, orchestra hits, "woo yeah"
samples, film dialogue, 808/909 hits, vinyl noise, dark pads, timestretch
artefacts.

### Arrangement

```
0-15    Intro: pads/atmosphere, filtered break, MC.
16-31   Break drops in, sub bass enters.
32-63   Main section: full break, sub, samples, MC.
64-79   Breakdown: break out, sub and atmosphere only.
80-111  Return: chopped harder, extra breaks layered.
112+    Outro or DJ rewind.
```

**The rewind** — literally spinning the record backwards and restarting a
section — is a genre convention, not a mistake.

## Breakbeat (the wider family)

| Style | Tempo | Feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Big beat** | 110–140 | Loud, distorted, rock-influenced; Chemical Brothers, Fatboy Slim |
| **Nu skool breaks** | 130–140 | Sound-design-heavy, techy, bass-driven |
| **Breaks / Florida breaks** | 128–135 | Party, bass-heavy, electro-influenced |
| **Progressive breaks** | 128–135 | Melodic, long-form |
| **Broken beat / bruk** | 120–135 | Jazzy, syncopated, West London |
| **Electro / breakdance** | 110–130 | 808-driven, robotic, funk |
| **Baltimore / Jersey club** | 130–140 | Chopped vocals, bed-squeak, triplet kicks |
| **Footwork / juke** | 155–165 | Triplet 808s, chopped soul vocals, minimal |
| **Miami bass** | 120–140 | 808 sub, party |
| **Trip hop** | 80–100 | Slow, dusty breaks, cinematic, melancholic |
| **Hardcore breakbeat** | 140–160 | Rave stabs, pianos, hoovers, 1991–93 |

### Big beat pattern
```
kick:   x - - - | - - x - | - - x - | - - - -
snare:  - - - - | x - - - | - - - - | x - - -
hat:    x . x . | x . x . | x . x . | x . x .
```
Plus: heavy compression, distortion, a filtered rock guitar or horn sample, and
a huge crash on every downbeat of an 8-bar phrase.

### Footwork pattern
```
kick:   x - - x | - - x - | - x - - | x - - -
snare/clap: - - - - | - - - - | x - - - | - - - -
808:    x - - x | - - x - | x - - x | - - x -     (triplet feel over 4/4)
```
Plus: a soul or R&B vocal chopped into single syllables, repeated obsessively.

## Production notes

- **Ghost notes are the soul of a break.** If your chopped break sounds
  mechanical, you have quantised away the quiet hits.
- **Layer a clean kick under the break's kick** for modern low end, and a clean
  snare under its snare for punch — the break supplies the texture, the one-shots
  supply the weight.
- High-pass the break at 100–200 Hz and let a dedicated sub carry the bottom.
- Parallel compression on the break makes the ghost notes audible.
- Bit-crushing and vinyl simulation are period-correct and still effective.
- **Timestretch artefacts** (the granular "smearing" of a stretched break) are a
  usable texture, not a bug.

## Legal note

Every classic break is a copyrighted recording. Sampling one commercially
requires clearance from both the recording owner and the composition owner.
Much of jungle's history is uncleared; that is a historical fact, not a legal
strategy. For released work, use royalty-free breaks, re-play the pattern with
your own drums, or clear the sample.

## Hazards

- Chopping so hard that the groove disappears — keep the kick/snare skeleton
  recognisable most of the time.
- Quantising the break to a rigid grid, killing its swing.
- No sub bass — jungle without weight is just fast drums.
- Modern loudness applied to a genre built on dynamics; leave headroom.
- Too many breaks layered — two or three, maximum, or the transients smear.
